## My Story

A little boy in the early ’90s, obsessed with the cheap monster truck VHS tapes my mom would bring home from the grocery store. I’d wear them out replaying the same scenes — Bigfoot, Grave Digger, Carolina Crusher, engines howling, dirt flying. I went to my first show in Salisbury, Maryland, sometime in the mid-’90s. I still remember the sound.

That sound changed something in me.  

The violent, beautiful roar of blown big blocks hitting the limiter — it was mechanical music. Every time I hear it, even now, it makes me smile. It reminds me of when monster trucks felt _alive_ — unpredictable, dangerous, and real.

Back then, the sport didn’t feel like a brand. It felt like a movement.  
Builders were inventing as they went, drivers were half-mad showmen, and every event felt like it might collapse under its own chaos. That was the magic: it wasn’t safe, or polished, or predictable — and that’s what made it worth loving.

As I got older, the trucks evolved — sleeker, faster, louder. I followed along through the VHS years into cable TV, then into the early Monster Jam era. I didn’t notice at first how much was changing. The bodies got flashier, the jumps higher, the crashes more deliberate. Somewhere in there, the _competition_ began to fade. The races that used to crown winners started to feel like intermissions before the big freestyle spectacle.

For a long time I ignored it. The sound was still there, the trucks still flew, and part of me wanted to believe it hadn’t really changed — that the heart of it was buried under the fluff. But over time the feeling wore thin. The shows got tighter, safer, cleaner. Everything that used to feel dangerous started feeling choreographed.

A few years ago, I tried again. I bought weekend tickets to a Monster Jam event — four shows across three days — thinking maybe I was being unfair, that seeing it live again would rekindle something. Instead, it confirmed everything. The tickets were overpriced, the commentary was shallow and cartoonish, and the trucks were treated like props in a fireworks act. Half the crowd was children waving branded flags, and every moment that might have turned risky was cut short by a kill switch or a corporate safety protocol.

Halfway through the second show, I got up and left. I wasn't angry — I was just done. I’d realized the sport I grew up loving hadn’t died a natural death. It had been tamed, cleaned up, and sold back to me as nostalgia. I still love the machines, the people who build them, the sound that started it all. But what’s left of the sport itself doesn’t ask for respect anymore. It asks for applause.

That was the moment I knew something deeper was wrong — not with the drivers or the fans, but with the system itself. What I’d loved about monster trucks wasn’t just the noise or the chaos; it was the sense of _freedom_ behind it. Every builder, every driver, every show used to be a little different, a little unpredictable. Somewhere along the line, that freedom got replaced by choreography.

I later learned I wasn’t imagining it. The transformation I felt from the stands was written into the sport’s own history. Freestyle competitions began as unscripted encores in the early ’90s but became the headliner by the 2000s. Everett Jasmer—the man behind USA-1 and the sport’s first champion—walked away when promoters turned competition into entertainment, the same realization I would have decades later from the stands.

And when Feld Entertainment, the same company that produces Disney on Ice, bought the series in 2008, the change became permanent. The television path tells the same story: from ESPN and TNN to increasingly niche channels like RACER. The sport that once drew millions of casual fans was slowly engineered for containment, not growth.

The sport didn’t die because people stopped caring. It died because its structure stopped rewarding the things that made it worth caring about. The danger, the creativity, the rivalry, the individuality — all the elements that made monster trucks a genuine motorsport — were slowly engineered out in favor of control, safety, and branding.

What’s left is still loud, but it’s hollow. It’s a mechanical circus that mistakes noise for meaning.

And it deserves better than that. It's a sport and a legacy that deserve respect.

Monster trucks were born from the same spirit that built hot rods and dirt tracks — a blue-collar rebellion against limitation. That spirit is still out there, scattered across small teams, YouTube clips, and fairground tours, waiting for a system that can make it matter again.

To understand what broke the sport—and how it might be rebuilt—you have to look at the structure itself.

## The Structural Problems

Monster trucks didn’t collapse overnight. They eroded through a series of design choices that gradually replaced competition with spectacle. Each decision made sense in isolation, but together they produced a system optimized for short-term thrills and long-term decay.  

At the center of all of it was freestyle—the rope the sport hanged itself with.

### **1. Centralization and Control**

When monster trucks were still a patchwork of regional tours and one-off shows, every promoter ran things a little differently. Rules, race formats, and track layouts varied wildly — one weekend you’d see side-by-side drag racing on a dirt strip, the next you’d see obstacle courses or time trials. That chaos was part of the charm. It was unpredictable, creative, and builder-driven.

This early, decentralized era was messy, unstandardized, and hard to package into a consistent, repeatable product — and that’s what Feld (and the promoters before them) sought to control.

For promoters trying to turn the sport into a repeatable, marketable product, that same freedom became a liability. Television deals and national sponsorships demanded consistency: a show that looked and felt the same city to city. Racing required infrastructure — timing systems, rule enforcement, qualified officials — and that left too many variables outside the promoter’s control. Freestyle solved all of that.

The shift wasn’t accidental. A racing-first format rewards teams that innovate — better suspension setups, lighter chassis, smarter drivers. A freestyle-first format rewards promoters who can choreograph chaos. Once the show became more important than the outcome, power naturally shifted away from the competitors and toward the people running the production.

From there, the sport began to behave less like motorsport and more like franchised entertainment. Promoters dictated which trucks appeared, how long they ran, even which ones were allowed to “win.” The independence that once defined monster trucks — a loose federation of builders experimenting with wild ideas — was replaced by a controlled roster of performers executing a script.

It made sense from a business perspective. Centralization made logistics cleaner, insurance easier, and sponsors happier. But the cost of that control was authenticity. The sport that was once a proving ground for creative mechanics became a managed spectacle designed to minimize variables. Every success story — from Bigfoot to USA-1 — came out of the wild-west era when anyone could show up, race hard, and earn respect. Under corporate consolidation, those pathways closed. You couldn’t build your way to the top anymore; you had to be invited.

That’s the quiet tragedy behind the modern show: the danger and individuality that built the sport were engineered out not by malice, but by management. Centralization didn’t just make the sport safer — it made it predictable. And predictability, in motorsport, is death.

### **2. Safety and Liability**

As the trucks grew faster, the stakes grew higher. Freestyle had turned broken parts into applause, but every new jump, flip, and crash also pushed the limits of what the machines — and the venues — could safely handle. At the same time, the sport was becoming more visible. Sponsors, insurers, and television partners all wanted bigger shows and cleaner outcomes. That tension — between escalation and containment — defined the modern era.

The paradox was simple: the audience came for chaos, but chaos is expensive and dangerous. Every crash risked a lawsuit; every rollover threatened not just a truck, but the viability of the whole event. To survive at a corporate scale, promoters had to make the product safer — not just for the drivers, but for the accountants.

The result was an arms race in the opposite direction. For every innovation that made the trucks more spectacular, another rule appeared to make them more manageable: kill switches, RPM limiters, standardized safety inspections, controlled course layouts. Each measure made sense on paper. Collectively, they drained the risk — and with it, the unpredictability — that had made the sport exciting in the first place.

Safety became another form of control. Promoters didn’t need to tell a driver how to perform if they could simply design the environment where certain outcomes were impossible. Engine rules, ramp geometry, and track dimensions all became invisible governors on creativity. Builders could still innovate, but only within a cage of compliance.

And yet, this wasn’t driven by cowardice or laziness. It was structural inevitability. Once monster trucks became a mass-market entertainment product, the legal and financial pressures made genuine danger intolerable. You can’t sell chaos in bulk. You can only simulate it.

That’s how a sport that once celebrated mechanical bravery turned into one that manufactures safety. The danger that once defined monster trucks didn’t disappear — it was outsourced to nostalgia.

### **3. Narrative Collapse**

Every real sport builds a story. Rivalries form, records are broken, champions rise and fall. That continuity gives fans something to care about — a reason to tune in next weekend, to argue who’s best, to remember who beat who. Monster trucks used to have that. The races meant something. Season points, head-to-head rivalries, and track records gave the chaos structure.

Freestyle erased all of it — or rather, replaced it with the _illusion_ of competition.  

On paper, Feld still runs points, leaderboards, even “championships.” But the scoring is built on applause, not performance. Racing still exists, but it barely matters. When 80% of a driver’s points come from freestyle and trick events, you stop rewarding precision and start rewarding theatrics.

The result is a sport that looks competitive but isn’t. The metrics are there, but they don’t measure anything real. You can’t crown a champion on the basis of subjective tricks and call it motorsport. It may be a competition, but it’s not a serious one — because “best stunt driver” isn’t a sporting title. It’s a performance note.

Some builders and drivers refused to play along. They hadn’t spent years perfecting engineering and craft just to smash their machines for applause. They built to compete — to measure themselves against something real — and when the sport stopped offering that, they walked away.

That’s why the modern leaderboard feels hollow. It gives the appearance of structure without consequence. You can’t build a real rivalry when the outcome depends on crowd reaction and arbitrary scoring systems. Fans sense that. They can feel when a “win” doesn’t mean anything.

So the stories stopped forming. Rivalries faded. Builders and drivers stopped being measured against one another in any meaningful way.  
What replaced it wasn’t a narrative — it was branding. The trucks became mascots, the drivers became interchangeable, and the competition became choreography.

That’s the heart of the narrative collapse. The machinery of sport is still there — brackets, trophies, leaderboards — but the meaning has drained out. The structure remains, but the story’s gone.

### **4. Economic Capture**

A judged sport built on destruction rewards whoever can afford to burn the most equipment. When freestyle took over, it created a perverse incentive structure: the most dramatic crashes and wildest runs — the ones that mangled suspensions and scattered fiberglass — earned the biggest cheers, the best scores, and eventually, the most bookings.

That loop turned competition into consumption. The teams with deeper pockets could afford to destroy more parts, rebuild faster, and roll into the next show looking fresh while smaller teams struggled just to make the next weekend. The result was a slow but inevitable _spending race_ — one where winning depended less on driving skill or mechanical creativity and more on who could shoulder the highest rate of attrition.

Freestyle, in other words, became a feedback loop that rewarded waste. Every broken part was marketing; every wrecked chassis, a commercial for who could afford to break another one next week. It was still thrilling to watch, but it hollowed out the middle class of the sport. Independent teams that couldn’t sustain those repair budgets either dropped out or stuck to smaller regional shows where competition still meant something.

For some, the price wasn’t just financial — it was philosophical. There were builders who refused to treat their machines as disposable props. They built to compete, not to destroy. They weren’t willing to turn craftsmanship into confetti for the sake of applause. In a system that rewarded recklessness over refinement, those kinds of people eventually had nowhere to fit.

Meanwhile, the rise of freestyle made _brands_ more valuable than _trucks_. In a world where the show mattered more than the outcome, a recognizable nameplate — “Grave Digger,” “Max-D,” “Megalodon” — became a more bankable asset than a well-built chassis or a skilled driver. Feld Entertainment understood this perfectly. They didn’t just dominate the circuit; they bought up the IP — the names, designs, and legacies — and folded them into a vertically integrated system where the company controlled the venues, the broadcast rights, the merchandise, and the rules themselves.

Independent operators were left with two options: go it alone in small-town arenas or sign contracts that effectively made them subcontractors inside Feld’s ecosystem. The company covered event logistics and paid appearance fees, but the teams still shouldered all the financial risks — trucks, transport, insurance, compliance — without ever owning the stage they performed on.

That’s what true economic capture looks like. The market didn’t eliminate the independents; the structure did. Freestyle economics rewarded unsustainable spending, and Feld’s consolidation rewarded compliance. The system evolved to protect the spectacle at the expense of the sport — until the only way to survive inside it was to surrender independence itself.

### **5. The Collapse of Fandom**

When the competition lost its meaning, the fans followed.

The sport didn’t just lose television coverage or sponsors — it lost **credibility**. Once freestyle became the headline act, the average fan no longer knew what they were watching. Was it a race? A stunt show? A demolition derby? The answer depended on what night you went.

In the early years, the appeal was simple: two trucks, one finish line, winner moves on. You didn’t need a rulebook. It was visceral, easy to follow, and — crucially — it created continuity. You could remember who beat who. When the format shifted toward judged performance, that clarity evaporated. Feld kept scoreboards and leaderboards, but the scoring was built mostly on subjective elements. Racing might count, but freestyle and tricks counted more. At that point the standings stopped feeling like a merit system and started feeling like a popularity ranking. It was still a competition — just not a serious one.

And as the format became more standardized, the type of stakes changed. Instead of “who’s the fastest tonight?” it became “will this truck hold together for the full run?” and “can this driver pull off what they’re trying to do?” That’s real jeopardy — mechanical, not mortal — and it’s one of the last honest thrills in the current version of the sport. Saves, weird landings, half-rolls brought back from the brink — those are still electric.

But the macro stakes were gone. A blown run didn’t affect a season. A truck breaking didn’t shift a championship. A driver having an off night didn’t reshape a rivalry. The show gave us momentary tension but no **accumulated consequence**. That’s fine for a 2 p.m. family show; it’s not how you keep adults arguing about trucks twenty years later.

At the same time, the broadcast tone shifted. Where the old ESPN/TNN coverage talked about engines, drivetrains, and track conditions, the modern style talked about characters, crowd energy, and merch. The sport stopped talking to the gearheads. And once you stop talking to the gearheads, you stop growing lifelong fans.

That’s the real collapse: monster trucks didn’t stop being impressive — they stopped being followable. Kids still love them. Adults ran out of reasons to stay.

### 6. The Identity Collapse

When monster trucks needed to differentiate themselves from other motorsports, they didn’t double down on engineering or competition — they doubled down on spectacle. The sport’s visual language drifted from “motorsport” toward “toy aisle.” Truck names became louder and more cartoonish. Announcers borrowed the tone of pro wrestling. Marketing leaned on words like DESTRUCTION and CHAOS until they lost meaning. It worked — for a while. It sold tickets, toys, and licensing deals.

But that choice came with a price. By defining itself through noise and exaggeration, the sport trained its own audience to see it as entertainment, not competition. Monster trucks became something parents took their kids to, not something adults debated over. When longtime fans tried to explain why they loved the sport, they had to argue against the sport’s own branding.

And Feld doubled down. Instead of cultivating distinct team identities, they standardized them. The same design tropes cycled endlessly: bulls, dogs, sharks, dragons, superheroes, pirates. Each one was a toy before it was a truck — an interchangeable product in a line. The builders and drivers became background actors in their own machines.

The presentation followed suit. Where other motorsports use commentary to deepen appreciation — Formula 1 with its engineering insight, MotoGP with its respect for bravery, NASCAR with its statistical storytelling — monster trucks went the opposite way. It told fans less. The technical and competitive substance was replaced with hype and catchphrases. Every show became an ad for itself.

The result is a kind of identity collapse. The sport no longer knows what it wants to be: racing? entertainment? live theater? It’s trapped in a visual and tonal language that makes seriousness impossible. The very term _monster truck_ now carries decades of branding baggage — so deeply tied to its own caricature that even when the trucks race, few people think of it as motorsport.

Monster trucks didn’t lose legitimacy because they failed to entertain. They lost it because they forgot how to be a sport while being entertaining.

## The Dream That Won’t Die

And yet, the fire never really went out.  

Even after the sport’s heart was paved over by licensing deals and scripted pyrotechnics, something real kept idling underneath. The big shows might have turned into theater, but the work never stopped. In garages, barns, and small-town machine shops, people are still building — still chasing that strange intersection of power, control, and creativity that made monster trucks worth caring about in the first place.

You can still find their work at fairgrounds, county arenas, and YouTube at 2 a.m. — low-res clips of trucks built on shoestring budgets, running harder than they should, sometimes breaking, sometimes winning, always trying. The crowds are smaller, the lighting worse, but the emotion is pure. That’s the culture that refuses to die.

The question isn’t whether that spirit still exists. It does. The question is whether anyone can design a system that protects and nourishes it — one that rewards creativity without crushing it, celebrates competition, and lets real risk exist without turning it into a circus.

That’s the challenge: not to resurrect the past, but to **rebuild the framework** that made it worth watching — to rebuild the structure without killing the spirit.

If the disease was structure, then the cure has to be structural too.

## The Proposed Solution

### Rebranding the Sport

**The Goal:** 
Create a brand identity that makes adult fans proud to be fans. One that treats monster trucks like the serious engineering and driving feat they actually are. One that says "motorsport" before it says "monster."

This doesn't mean stripping away personality or making it boring. It means letting the real drama—the competition, the creativity, the danger—carry the narrative instead of manufacturing artificial hype.

#### A New Nomenclature: The Unlimited 4x4 Classification

**The Problem:**  
"Monster truck" may be permanently tainted. The term is indelibly associated with children's entertainment, sideshows, and the exact spectacle-over-sport aesthetic that killed the competitive legitimacy. For many potential fans and sponsors, "monster truck" signals something fundamentally unserious.

**The Strategy:**  
Introduce **"Unlimited 4x4"** as the official classification for these vehicles in all formal contexts—rules, results, technical specifications, team documentation. This isn't about erasing history or pretending these aren't monster trucks. It's about establishing a parallel *professional designation that signals legitimacy*.

**How It Works:**

- **Official/Technical:** All rules, vehicle specs, and results refer to "Unlimited 4x4 vehicles" or "U4x4 class"
- **Marketing/Public:** Use "Unlimited 4x4 racing" prominently; let "monster truck" appear contextually when helpful for search/recognition
- **Team Identity:** Encourage teams to brand themselves as "Unlimited 4x4" operations

**Precedent:**  
Other motorsports do this naturally:

- People say "Indy cars" but the series calls them "open-wheel race cars"
- NASCAR fans say "stock cars" but the sanctioning body uses "Cup Series vehicles" or "Next Gen cars"
- NHRA uses technical classifications (Top Fuel, Funny Car) that started as slang but became official

**The Technical Justification:**  
These machines aren't trucks in any meaningful sense. They're purpose-built, tube-frame racing chassis with fiberglass bodies. Calling them "Unlimited 4x4" is more technically accurate—it describes what they are (four-wheel drive competition vehicles with minimal restrictions) rather than what they look like.

**The Benefit:**  
When potential sponsors, media partners, or fans hear "Unlimited 4x4," they're hearing a motorsport class designation. It sounds professional, technical, and legitimate. It sidesteps decades of baggage while remaining perfectly accurate.

**Example Usage:**

- _Casual/accessible:_ "The Unlimited 4x4 Championship brings back real monster truck racing—legitimate Unlimited 4x4 competition with real stakes."
- _Official program:_ "Tonight's feature: 16 Unlimited 4x4 vehicles competing in single-elimination racing for a $500,000 grand prize."
- _Technical doc:_ "All Unlimited 4x4 vehicles must meet minimum weight (10,000 lbs), tire size (66"), and safety requirements..."

The goal isn't to make people stop saying "monster truck." The goal is to give the sport a professional designation that doesn't carry the weight of failed branding decisions. Over time, as the classification gains recognition in competition contexts, it becomes the term serious fans and participants use—while casual observers can still call them whatever they want.

#### Re-Establish Legitimacy Through Presentation

**The Problem:**  
Monster truck branding became trapped in a visual and tonal language that undermines legitimacy. Cartoonish truck names, destruction-focused marketing, and carnival barker announcing trained audiences to see it as a spectacle rather than a sport.

**The Fix:**

**1. Visual Identity**

- Clean, modern design language that emphasizes engineering and competition
- Photography that highlights the machinery, the builders, the craft—not cartoonish or garish imagery or styling
- Avoid the "extreme sports" aesthetic from the early 2000s (already dated)
- Think more like motorsport documentary than monster truck poster

**2. Naming and Language**

- Vehicle names can be creative, but they should sound like race teams, not cartoon characters
- Avoid overuse of "EXTREME," "INSANE," "DESTRUCTION" in all marketing
- Announcers should have technical literacy and treat the sport with respect
- Use motorsport language: "drivers," "teams," "engineers," not "performers"

**3. Content Tone**

- Celebrate the craft: fabrication, materials, suspension geometry, engine tuning, driver skill
- Show the human side: builders in their shops, families supporting teams, the grind of preparation
- Let the danger speak for itself—you don't need to hype it
- Authenticity over production value: raw, honest, real

**4. Comparison Points**

- **What to avoid:** Traditional monster truck rally ads and radio spots, WWE production style, circus aesthetics
- **What to emulate:** MotoGP's respect for riders, F1's engineering focus, dirt track racing's grassroots authenticity

#### Team Identity Over Truck Identity 

For decades, monster trucks have branded around the vehicle itself—the name, the paint scheme, the character. That made sense in an era where trucks were marketed as toys and the goal was shelf space at Walmart. A memorable truck name sold lunch boxes and Hot Wheels.

But that branding strategy creates a problem for legitimacy. When the truck is the brand—especially when that brand is themed around cartoons, food mascots, or fictional characters—it's hard to position the sport as serious competition. Adults don't follow mascot-vs-mascot matchups the way they follow racing operations, driver legacies, and team histories.

Real motorsports brand differently. You don't root for "the red car with the horse logo"—you root for Ferrari, for Scuderia's engineering legacy, for the drivers who've worn the suit. The vehicle is the tool; the team is the identity.

The Unlimited 4x4 model should encourage teams to brand as operations first. The TEAM becomes the professional entity—the name people recognize, follow, and respect. The truck they're fielding can still have a name, a theme, a visual identity, but it's not doing all the branding work.

This isn't about erasing personality or forcing sterile corporate branding. It's about creating a structure where the people, the craft, and the competition come first—and the spectacle follows naturally. When fans know the builders, respect the drivers, and follow the teams, the sport earns credibility. When they only know cartoon mascots, it stays in the toy aisle.

Teams that want to lead with truck branding can still do that. But the championship itself should emphasize team operations, driver skill, and engineering credibility in its presentation. Let the substance build the audience, not just the flash.

### Unlimited 4x4 Championship
#### Purpose — Why It Exists

**Mission:**  
Rebuild monster trucking as a legitimate motorsport and cultural movement — restoring authenticity, competition, and engineering creativity after decades of corporate sterilization.

**Core Philosophy:**

- Respect the _builder_, not the brand.
- Reward _competition_, not choreography.
- Celebrate _skill_, not destruction.
- Build a _system_, not a spectacle.

**Goal:**  
Prove that independent teams, smart promotion, and clear rules can produce a fan-driven, sponsor-worthy event that reignites adult interest in the sport.
#### Structure — How the Event Works

**1. One Big Annual Event**

- Held in a central U.S. location (Tennessee, Kentucky, Missouri corridor).
- Position it as the _Super Bowl for independent monster trucks_ — one massive weekend, not a tour.
- Easier logistics, focused hype, one narrative anchor each year.

**2. The Competition Format**

- **Primary event:** Qualifying, then racing (objective, timed or head-to-head).
- **Secondary:** Optional freestyle showcase _after_ racing — purely exhibition, no effect on podium results.
- Restores legitimacy and clarity while preserving entertainment value.

**3. The Prize Pool (Proposed)**

- $1,000,000 total purse:
    - 1st: $500k
    - 2nd: $250k
    - 3rd: $125k
    - 4th: $75k
    - 5th: $50k
- Pays deep enough to reward smaller teams for showing up.
- Signals seriousness — real stakes, real respect.

**4. Rule Philosophy**

- Keep rules minimal but enforce objectivity:
    - Racing must have measurable outcomes (timing, penalties, etc.).
    - Mechanical regulations focus on safety and parity, not corporate spec kits.
- Preserve the “freedom to innovate” within reason — allow multiple drivetrain configurations, engine types, chassis designs.

#### Media Strategy — How It Lives Beyond the Arena

**1. “Competent, Not Corporate” Media**

- Prioritize _authenticity over gloss_: handheld cameras, clear sound, honest editing.
- Weekly teaser content (team intros, shop visits, engineering explainers).
- Mic’d-up pit footage and raw post-race reactions — fans want the _garage energy._

**2. Six-Month Hype Cycle**

- **January:** Announcement & trailer drop.
- **Feb–May:** Team features, engineering spotlights, mini-docs.
- **June:** The event itself.
- **Post-event:** Edited YouTube releases — highlights cut (30min) + full race broadcast (90min).

**3. Find a Voice**

- Secure an announcer/commentator with both technical literacy and charisma (someone in the Scott Douglass mold, or a motorsports YouTuber who “gets it”).
- Consistency of voice builds brand identity.

**4. Team-Driven Promotion**

- Equip each team with a shared _media kit_: logos, hashtags, templates, social snippets.
- Encourage them to market themselves — “distributed storytelling.”
- Reward top social performers (e.g., “Fan Engagement Award”). 
	- Cash reward if possible.

#### Economics — How to Make It Viable

**1. Simplified Cost Structure**

- One event means one set of logistics — cheaper and easier to secure sponsors for.
- Partner with an existing fairground or motorsport park with dirt infrastructure.

**2. Tiered Sponsorship Model**

- Title Sponsor ($250k)
- Major Class Sponsors ($100k each)
- Supporting Sponsors ($25–50k)
- Regional / equipment sponsors ($5–10k each)  
    → diversified and attainable, not dependent on one corporate backer.

**3. Alternative Revenue Streams**

- **Crowdfunded purse booster:** Fans can contribute directly to the prize pool (“Your ticket fuels the purse”).
- **Streaming partnership:** Partner with YouTube-native production teams (e.g., Donut Media, Cleetus McFarland, 1320video, or Hoonigan).
- **Pre-event merch sales:** “Founding Year” drop to test demand and seed budget.

**4. “Proof-of-Concept” Year**

- Frame Year 1 as a _prototype_ to prove the model’s financial and cultural viability.
- Transparency builds fan trust (“Here’s where your money went — to the teams and to production.”)

#### Culture — What It Stands For

**1. Long Leash, Short Fuse Policy**

- Drivers and teams are encouraged to be authentic — rough edges and all.
- Only hard lines: hate speech, harassment, or bringing the sport into disrepute.
- Authenticity > PR polish.

**2. No Scripts. No Safety Nets. Unlimited 4x4.**

- Tagline ideas: 
    - _Independent. Unfiltered. Unstoppable._
    - *Hand Built. Raced Hard.*
    - *Real Machines. Real Competition.* 

**3. Engineering Literacy**

- Feature builders, fabricators, and mechanics as _characters_, not background crew.
- Include mini-segments or short interviews: “Shop Talk,” “The Science of Suspension,” “Inside the Chassis.”
- Possibly bring in Marty Garza for historical + technical segments — make him the sport’s “philosopher-engineer.”

**4. Blue-Collar Reverence, Not Caricature**

- Honor the builders’ craft.
- Avoid the cheap “yee-haw” stereotype that’s haunted the sport for decades.
- Treat the audience like _smart adults_ who understand machinery and competition.

#### Long-Term Vision for the Event

**Phase 1 (Year 1–2):**

- Prove viability with one flagship event, strong content, and fan trust.
- Build a recognizable aesthetic and cultural tone — gritty, respectful, mature.

**Phase 2 (Year 3–5):**

- Expand to a _small circuit_ (3–4 regional events feeding into the annual championship).
- Create a points system, establishing season-long rivalries and fan investment.

**Phase 3 (Beyond):**

- Develop a streaming platform or partnership that showcases independent motorsports under one umbrella.
- Position the Unlimited 4x4 Championship as the _anchor event_ of a revitalized independent motorsport ecosystem — monster trucks, pulling, dirt drags, hill climbs, etc.

#### Core Narrative 

> _The Unlimited 4x4 Championship isn’t nostalgia — it’s reform._  
> It’s about proving that monster trucks can still be a sport, not a circus.  
> That competition still matters.  
> That freedom, danger, and creativity are not liabilities — they’re the reason this ever mattered at all.

### Why This Is Hard (And Why It's Worth Trying Anyway)

Anyone reading this can already see the problems. Building an independent monster truck event that challenges the established order isn't just difficult—it's borderline absurd. But if we're going to have an honest conversation about fixing this sport, we need to acknowledge what stands in the way.

#### **1. "Monster Jam Will Crush You"**

**The Reality:**  
Monster Jam has the brands, the venues, the sponsors, the infrastructure, and decades of momentum. Feld Entertainment is a billion-dollar company that runs Disney on Ice and supercross. They have relationships with every major arena and fairground in North America. They own or license most of the recognizable truck names.

They could absolutely make life difficult for any competing event—pressuring venues not to book you, discouraging teams from participating, or simply outspending you into irrelevance.

**Why It's Not a Dealbreaker:**  
Monster Jam abandoned a huge segment of their original audience when they pivoted to family entertainment. This isn't about competing with them for the same crowd—it's about serving the fans they don't want anymore. The adults who walked out. The gearheads who want competition. The people still watching grainy YouTube videos at 2 a.m.

You're not trying to beat Disney on Ice. You're trying to build something for the people who never wanted Disney on Ice in the first place.

**The Honest Limitation:**  
Teams who participate might face retaliation—being quietly blacklisted from Monster Jam events or losing sponsorship opportunities. That's a real risk, and it's not one I can solve by writing a manifesto.

#### **2. "The Insurance and Liability Are Impossible"**

**The Reality:**  
Monster trucks are 12,000-pound machines with supercharged engines that regularly go airborne and occasionally flip. The insurance premiums are brutal. The liability waivers are extensive. One serious injury could bankrupt the entire operation—or worse.

This is probably the single biggest logistical nightmare facing any independent event.

**Why It's Not a Dealbreaker:**  
Small independent monster truck shows happen all over the country right now. Pulling events, tractor shows, and dirt track racing all deal with similar risks. It's expensive and complicated, but it's not impossible. People are doing it—just on a smaller scale.

The key is working with experienced motorsport insurance brokers, adopting proven safety protocols, and being transparent about the risks with both teams and fans.

**The Honest Limitation:**  
I don't have a magic solution here. Insurance will be one of the highest costs, and one bad incident could end everything. This is the part that keeps me up at night.

#### **3. "There Aren't Enough Independent Teams"**

**The Reality:**  
The independent monster truck ecosystem fragmented over the years. Many builders gave up or went dormant when the cost of competing in Monster Jam became prohibitive. The teams that remain are scattered across small regional circuits with varying levels of competitiveness.

**Why It's Not a Dealbreaker:**  
They didn't disappear—they just stopped having a reason to show up. Regional circuits still operate. Teams still build trucks. A recent article mentioned 10-12 competitive teams in the Rocky Mountain area alone.

A legitimate competition with a serious prize purse might bring builders out of retirement or convince existing teams to invest in a second truck. The question isn't whether the teams exist—it's whether they'd commit.

**The Honest Limitation:**  
I don't actually know how many competitive trucks could show up for an event like this. It might be 8. It might be 20. That uncertainty is part of why Year 1 would be a gamble.

An entire generation of fans checked out when monster trucks became a kids' show. They moved on to NASCAR, dirt track racing, or just stopped caring. Convincing them to come back after 20 years of disappointment is a hard sell.

**Why It's Not a Dealbreaker:**  
But they didn't stop loving what monster trucks _were_. My walkout proves it—people still show up hoping to find the sport they remember, only to leave disappointed. The nostalgic YouTube views prove people remember. The fact that Everett Jasmer's story still gets told proves the hunger for authenticity never died.

Latent nostalgia is real. The question is whether it can be converted into ticket sales.

**The Honest Limitation:**  
This is unproven. The market might be smaller than I think, or it might not exist at all. I could be projecting my own frustration onto an audience that's actually fine with what Monster Jam has become.

That's an enormous number with no guaranteed revenue stream behind it. Ticket sales from a single event won't cover it. Sponsorships at that level require connections and credibility that a first-year event doesn't have. Crowdfunding might help, but it won't get you to seven figures.

**Why It's Not a Dealbreaker:**

## Protecting Independence: The Team-Owned Model
### **1. The Problem That Success Creates**

If this idea works—if an independent Unlimited 4x4 event proves that serious competition can draw crowds, attract sponsors, and sustain teams—it immediately becomes valuable. And the moment it becomes valuable, it becomes a target.

And not necessarily a target for hostility or malice. Maybe a well-meaning investor wants to "help the sport scale." Maybe a media company offers a lucrative broadcast deal that comes with creative control. Maybe a corporate sponsor wants naming rights and a say in the format. The pressure doesn't always look like a threat. Sometimes it looks like opportunity. But the result is the same: the thing that made it work—independence, authenticity, competitive integrity—gets traded away for growth, stability, and profit.

Success without structural protection is just a countdown timer.

#### The Consolidation Pattern

This isn't speculation. It's how markets work. Independent ventures that prove viable get absorbed, replicated, or pressured into consolidation. The pattern is predictable:

1. **Independence:** A small group builds something real, driven by passion and principle.
2. **Success:** It works. Fans show up. Money flows.
3. **Consolidation:** A larger entity—whether through acquisition, competition, or partnership—moves to control it.
4. **Optimization:** The new controllers optimize for their goals: predictability, cost management, scalability, return on investment.
5. **Erosion:** The qualities that made it valuable in the first place (risk, creativity, independence) become expensive liabilities to manage. They get engineered out.

The people running the consolidation aren't villains. They're doing what any rational business entity does: maximizing value and minimizing risk. But maximizing value in entertainment means turning art into product, and minimizing risk in motorsport means turning danger into theater.

The incentives that made the thing successful—letting teams innovate, allowing real stakes, rewarding competitive merit—directly conflict with the incentives that make it profitable at scale. Freedom is expensive. Authenticity is unpredictable. Real competition creates winners and losers, and losers are bad for branding.

So the freedom gets managed. The authenticity gets polished. The competition gets balanced for entertainment value. And within a few years, you're right back where you started.

#### This Has Happened Before—Repeatedly

Take American open-wheel racing. In the 1990s, CART (Championship Auto Racing Teams) was thriving—a team-owned series where the people who built and raced the cars controlled the sport. Races were competitive, teams were profitable, and the Indy 500 was the crown jewel. Then Tony George, who controlled Indianapolis Motor Speedway, wanted more control over the sport itself. He launched the Indy Racing League in 1996 as a competing series, fracturing open-wheel racing into two warring factions. The split lasted 12 years. Sponsors fled. TV ratings collapsed. Both series bled money and credibility. By the time they reunified in 2008, the damage was irreversible—NASCAR had eaten their lunch, and open-wheel racing in America never recovered its cultural relevance. The lesson? When ownership and control become contested, the sport itself loses. CART worked because the teams owned it. The moment that structure broke, everything else broke with it.

Or look at the UFC. In the early 2000s, mixed martial arts was a patchwork of regional promotions—Pride in Japan, Strikeforce, WEC, and dozens of smaller shows. The UFC systematically bought them all, absorbed their talent, and dismantled the competition. Now they control the fighters, the IP, the broadcast rights, and the rulebook. Independent promotions exist, but only in the gaps the UFC doesn't care about. Fighters have almost no leverage. It's not a federation of competitors—it's a vertically integrated entertainment company that happens to feature fighting.

We could examine boxing's promoter wars, where matchmaking serves business interests over competitive merit. Formula 1's ongoing tension between teams and Liberty Media, where revenue splits and governance remain perpetual battlegrounds. The corporatization of the X Games, which sanitized action sports into family-friendly programming. WWE's near-monopoly on pro wrestling until AEW finally offered an alternative. Live Nation's consolidation of music festivals, turning independent events into nodes in a corporate portfolio.

**The pattern repeats because the incentives are structural, not personal.** When something independent proves valuable, capital moves to control it. When one entity consolidates power, optimization follows—and optimization in entertainment means predictability, cost control, and risk mitigation. The very things that made the product valuable (danger, creativity, independence) become liabilities to manage.

**Monster trucks have already lived this story once.** The wild-west era—when anyone could show up with a truck, race hard, and earn respect—gave way to corporate consolidation. Feld Entertainment didn't destroy the sport out of malice; they optimized it for their business model. Centralized control made logistics easier, liability more manageable, and the product more consistent. It also made the sport predictable, scripted, and hollow. That transformation wasn't an accident or a failure of vision. It was the inevitable result of one entity controlling the infrastructure, the rules, the brands, and the revenue.

If you build a successful independent alternative without addressing that structural vulnerability, you're not creating a solution. You're just creating the next problem—the next thing that will eventually need escaping from.

#### The Stakes

The teams who would invest in this—who would build trucks, commit their time, risk their reputations—deserve to know that this can't be sold out from under them. That five years from now, when the event is profitable and attracting attention, some well-funded entity can't swoop in, buy the rights, and turn it back into exactly what everyone was trying to escape.

Fans who show up deserve to know that the competition they're supporting won't morph into another choreographed spectacle the moment it becomes commercially viable.

And builders—the people who've kept the independent spirit alive in garages and small-town shows—deserve infrastructure they can trust. A system that rewards their work instead of absorbing it.

**So the question isn't just "how do we build a better event?"**

The question is: **"How do we build something that can't be corrupted the same way?"**

### **2. The Cooperative Model: An Overview**

The solution is structural: **teams collectively own and govern the championship.**

Not "teams participate in an event someone else runs." Not "teams have input on decisions." Teams own the entity. They control the rules, the revenue, the growth strategy, and the competitive format. The Unlimited 4x4 Championship—or whatever it's called—exists as a member-owned cooperative where the competitors are the stakeholders.

This isn't just a governance tweak. It's a fundamentally different power structure.

#### How It's Different From Traditional Sanctioning Bodies

In most motorsports, the sanctioning body is a separate corporate entity that controls the sport and the teams participate under its authority.

NASCAR is owned by the France family through NASCAR Holdings. They set the rules, control the schedule, negotiate broadcast deals, and distribute revenue as they see fit. Teams are independent businesses, but they operate within a system someone else owns. If NASCAR decides to change the playoff format, add more road courses, or shift the revenue split—teams can complain, but they don't get a vote.

IndyCar operates under a similar structure. The series is owned by Penske Entertainment, which also owns Indianapolis Motor Speedway and several teams. Roger Penske controls the commercial rights, the rulebook, and the calendar. Teams compete, but they don't govern.

In both cases, the teams are **participants in someone else's system**. They can negotiate, lobby, and threaten to leave—but ultimate control rests with the entity that owns the series. That entity's incentives—maximizing profit, securing broadcast deals, managing liability—don't always align with what's best for competition or long-term sporting health.

The current monster truck model is even more extreme. Feld Entertainment doesn't just own the series—they own most of the truck brands, control the venues, manage the broadcasts, and employ drivers as contractors. Teams that operate independently do so in the margins, at smaller shows with lower pay and less visibility. There's no separation between the promoter and the product. Feld is the ecosystem.

**The cooperative model inverts this entirely.** The teams don't participate in someone else's system—they ARE the system. The championship exists as a legal entity owned collectively by its members (the racing teams). Governance is democratic. Revenue is shared. Major decisions require consensus. No single person or corporation can sell the series, change the rules unilaterally, or optimize the sport for goals that conflict with competitive integrity.

It's the difference between renting and owning. In a traditional sanctioning body, teams rent access to the infrastructure. In a cooperative, they own the infrastructure.

#### Why Formula 1 Teams Have Power

Formula 1 operates under a unique structure that, while not a true cooperative, demonstrates why distributed ownership and collective bargaining matter.

F1 is owned commercially by Liberty Media, which controls the broadcast rights, sponsorships, and event promotion. But the teams—through decades of negotiation and leverage—secured a seat at the table. They don't own the series, but they do own their participation in it.

The Concorde Agreement, renegotiated roughly every decade, is the contract that governs the relationship between the teams, the FIA (the sport's regulator), and Formula One Management (the commercial rights holder). It establishes:

- **Revenue distribution**: Teams collectively receive approximately 63% of F1's commercial revenue, divided by performance, historical value, and participation.
- **Governance input**: Major rule changes require team consultation or supermajority approval. Liberty Media can't unilaterally redesign the sport.
- **Collective leverage**: Teams negotiate as a bloc. If they don't sign the Concorde Agreement, there's no championship.

This structure exists because the teams learned early that individual negotiations left them powerless. If Ferrari negotiates alone, Liberty Media can play them against McLaren. But if all ten teams negotiate together, they have leverage. They can threaten a breakaway series (and have, multiple times). That threat forces the commercial rights holder to share power.

The result is a system where the incentives are—imperfectly, but meaningfully—aligned. Liberty Media wants a profitable, popular championship. The teams want competitive legitimacy and financial sustainability. Neither can get what they want without the other, so they're forced to compromise.

F1 teams don't control the sport outright, but they have enough power to prevent it from being optimized into irrelevance. Rule changes that would hurt competition get blocked. Revenue splits that would starve smaller teams get renegotiated. The system isn't perfect—it's still too expensive, still too politicized—but it's survivable, because the people racing have a structural say in how the sport is run.

#### The Core Principle: The People Who Race Control the Sport

The cooperative model takes that principle further. Instead of teams negotiating for a share of power within someone else's system, they own the system outright.

Decisions about rules, competition formats, revenue distribution, and long-term strategy are made by the people with the most skin in the game—the ones building the trucks, paying the entry fees, risking their equipment, and carrying the sport's reputation. Not by a distant corporate office optimizing for quarterly earnings. Not by a promoter whose incentives favor spectacle over competition.

The people who race. Period.

This doesn't mean chaos or endless arguments. It means accountability flows in the right direction. If a rule change hurts competition, the competitors can block it. If a format prioritizes entertainment over sport, the teams who actually have to perform under it get a vote. If revenue distribution doesn't sustain smaller teams, the members can fix it—because it's their system, and its failure is their failure.

**So what would that actually look like in practice?**

### **3. How It Would Work**

This isn't a complete constitution—drafting that would require lawyers, accountants, and input from actual founding teams. But here are the core structural elements that would need to exist to make the cooperative model functional and protected.

#### Governance Structure

One team, one vote. Period.

It doesn't matter if you're a legacy operation that's been building trucks for 30 years or a first-year team running on a shoestring budget. It doesn't matter if your truck is famous or if your driver is a household name. Every member team gets equal say in how the championship is run.

This isn't idealism—it's protection. The moment you allow weighted voting based on tenure, funding, or popularity, you create classes. And once you have classes, you have incentives for the top tier to optimize the system for themselves at the expense of everyone else. Equal voting prevents capture by elites and ensures decisions reflect the collective interest, not just the loudest or richest voices.

Major decisions—rule changes, format adjustments, new broadcast deals, entry requirements, revenue distribution formulas—require a supermajority vote, likely 70-75%. This threshold is high enough to prevent slim majorities from ramming through changes that half the membership opposes, but low enough that the system doesn't become paralyzed by a single holdout. It forces consensus-building. If you can't convince three-quarters of your fellow competitors that a change is good for the sport, it probably isn't.

What counts as "major"? Anything that affects competition structure, financial sustainability, or the cooperative's mission. Day-to-day operations—event logistics, marketing campaigns, vendor contracts—can be handled by hired management. But the decisions that shape what the sport is and who it serves stay with the members.

#### Revenue Sharing Model

The revenue model needs to balance two goals: rewarding performance and sustaining participation.

Prize money stays merit-based. The winner gets the biggest share, second place gets less, and so on down the field. That's how competition works. You don't distribute trophies evenly, and you don't distribute prize money evenly either. The purse rewards the best performance on race day.

But operational revenue—the money the championship generates from ticket sales, broadcast rights, title sponsorships, and other commercial activity—gets distributed differently. That money doesn't just belong to the winners. It belongs to everyone who made the event viable: the teams who showed up, built the field, took the risk, and gave the fans something worth watching.

A reasonable split might be 60% distributed based on performance (finishing position, points, championships) and 40% distributed equally to all participating teams. Or 70/30. Or 80/20. The exact ratio matters less than the principle: everyone who participates gets a share of the collective success, but those who perform better get more.

This structure incentivizes both showing up and competing hard. A team that finishes mid-pack still walks away with something—enough to justify the expense of entry, maybe enough to reinvest in next year's truck. A team that wins big gets rewarded significantly. And crucially, the teams that build the championship into something valuable share in that value.

There's one important constraint: the championship entity itself needs to retain enough revenue to operate sustainably. Events cost money—venue rentals, insurance, safety crews, officials, media production. If 100% of revenue gets distributed to teams, the cooperative goes bankrupt and there's no Year 2. A reasonable floor might be reserving 20-30% of total revenue for operational costs and future growth, with the remainder split among the teams. This number would need to be determined by actual budgets and agreed upon by the membership, but the principle stands: the entity needs enough to survive, and teams split the rest.

As the championship grows and more teams join, individual revenue shares will naturally dilute—but total revenue should grow faster than membership if the model is working. More teams mean bigger fields, better competition, more compelling broadcasts, and higher commercial value. A team's share might shrink slightly in percentage terms while still growing in absolute dollars. And if a team can't remain viable even with revenue sharing, that's competition working as intended. The cooperative creates fair infrastructure; it doesn't guarantee survival for every participant.

#### New Team Entry

The cooperative can't be a closed club. If independent teams can't join, you've just recreated exclusivity under a different name.

Entry criteria need to be objective and transparent:

- **Safety standards**: Roll cage certification, fire suppression, driver restraints, kill switches—all the non-negotiable stuff that keeps people alive.
- **Equipment requirements**: Minimum and maximum specs (weight, tire size, engine type) that ensure competitive parity without forcing everyone into identical builds.
- **Financial commitment**: Proof you can afford to compete—entry fees, insurance, transport. Not a wealth test, but a solvency check. The sport can't carry teams that can't carry themselves.
- **Operational reliability**: Can you show up to events on time, maintain your equipment, and compete without being a safety liability?
- **Ownership disclosure**: Full transparency about who owns and controls the team—individuals, LLCs, parent companies, investors, everything.

Teams that meet these standards are presumed approved. There's no gatekeeping vote where existing members can block qualified competitors just to protect their revenue share. If you meet the criteria, you're in. That's how you avoid turning into Formula 1, where incumbent teams vote down new entries to maintain their slice of the pie.

However, the cooperative reserves the right to challenge and investigate any team's independence. Any member can raise concerns about potential affiliation violations, and the challenged team must provide documentation. Refusal to disclose is grounds for automatic expulsion.

A buy-in fee is reasonable. Joining the cooperative means gaining ownership in its infrastructure, its brand, and its future revenue. That stake has value, and new members should contribute to the collective investment. But the fee needs to be calibrated carefully—high enough to be meaningful, low enough that it doesn't become a barrier that only wealthy teams can clear. Maybe it's $10,000. Maybe it's $25,000. Maybe it scales based on the championship's financial health. The point is that it funds the system's growth, not that it keeps people out.

#### Anti-Affiliation Rules

The biggest threat to the cooperative isn't direct acquisition—it's infiltration. A corporate entity like Feld doesn't need to buy the championship if they can simply create enough "independent" teams to control the votes from the inside.

The bylaws must explicitly prohibit affiliated entities from holding multiple memberships. One ownership entity gets one vote, period.

"Affiliation" needs a clear legal definition:

- **Common ownership**: If the same person, company, or parent organization owns more than 25% of multiple teams, those teams are affiliated.
- **Shared management**: If teams share executive leadership, operational control, or decision-making authority, they're affiliated.
- **Coordinated control**: If teams demonstrably collude on votes, sponsor deals, or competitive decisions, they're affiliated.

If affiliation is proven, all affiliated teams are expelled. Their memberships are revoked, their stakes revert to the cooperative, and they forfeit any claim to future revenue. No refunds, no appeals, no second chances.

Any member can bring forward evidence of suspected affiliation. The accused teams get an opportunity to respond and provide documentation. A supermajority vote is required to expel, which prevents frivolous accusations from being weaponized. But the burden of proof is on the accused—if you can't demonstrate independence, you're out.

Teams must also report any ownership changes within 30 days. Selling part of your team to an investor? Bringing on a new partner? Restructuring your LLC? The cooperative needs to know. Failure to disclose is a breach of the membership agreement and grounds for expulsion.

This isn't paranoia. It's structural defense. Without these rules, a sufficiently motivated corporate actor could flood the membership, gain voting control, and turn the cooperative into a puppet system within a few years. The anti-affiliation rules make that strategy detectable, expensive, and ultimately futile.

#### What Can and Can't Be Sold

This is where the protection actually lives.

Each team owns their own truck name, branding, and intellectual property individually. If you built "Thunder Run" or "Iron Menace" or whatever, that's yours. You control the merch, the sponsorships, the social media. You can license it, sell it, or retire it. The cooperative doesn't own your identity.

But the championship entity itself—the "Unlimited 4x4 Championship" or whatever it's called—cannot be sold to a corporate buyer. Not to a promoter. Not to a media company. Not to a private equity firm. The structure is member-owned, and it stays member-owned. If someone wants to invest in the championship, they can sponsor it. They can't buy it.

Individual membership stakes also can't be sold to non-racing entities. You can't sell your seat in the cooperative to a corporation that wants to install a puppet team and gain voting power. If a team leaves—whether they retire, dissolve, or get expelled for violating bylaws—their membership stake reverts to the cooperative. It either gets redistributed among remaining members or allocated to a new team that meets entry criteria. The cooperative decides, not the departing member.

This prevents infiltration. Without these rules, a well-funded corporation could simply buy up enough membership stakes to control the votes, and you're right back to centralized control under a cooperative façade.

#### Sponsorship Standards

Teams own their brands and choose their own sponsors—but not without limits.

The cooperative's bylaws need to establish prohibited sponsor categories: adult content, tobacco, certain controlled substances, and any other categories the membership agrees would damage the championship's commercial viability or public reputation. These standards protect the collective. One team's controversial sponsor could cost everyone else their broadcast deal, title sponsor, or venue access.

This isn't heavy-handed moralism. It's practical self-preservation. If a team wants to run a PornHub livery, that's their right—but not within a cooperative where their decision affects everyone else's income.

Violation of sponsorship standards is automatic grounds for expulsion. Teams discovered in violation receive notice and a brief cure period (perhaps 30 days) to remove the prohibited sponsorship. Failure to comply results in immediate termination of membership. Teams that show up to an event with a banned sponsor despite prior warnings are disqualified on the spot and expelled without further process.

The membership can revisit and adjust these standards through supermajority vote. If the sport's culture or commercial landscape changes, the bylaws can change with it. But the principle remains: individual freedom exists within community standards that protect collective viability.

#### Competition Primacy

The cooperative's bylaws need to state explicitly: **sporting integrity takes precedence over entertainment value.** 

This isn't a vague mission statement. It's a binding principle. Any proposed rule change, format adjustment, or commercial deal has to be evaluated first on whether it serves competitive fairness. If it doesn't—if it prioritizes spectacle, audience appeal, or sponsor demands over legitimate competition—it gets rejected.

This doesn't mean the sport has to be boring or inaccessible. It means the competition comes first, and the entertainment follows from watching real stakes play out. You don't manufacture drama. You let the racing create it.

This clause exists to prevent the slow drift toward spectacle that killed the sport the first time. It's a structural safeguard that says: we will not trade authenticity for profit, even when it's tempting, even when it would be easier.

**So Why Make It This Complicated?**

### **4. Why This Is Harder (And Why It's Worth It)**

The cooperative model is harder to launch than a traditional event. Significantly harder.

A single promoter with vision, capital, and connections could theoretically organize an independent monster truck race in a year. Secure a venue, set a purse, invite teams, sell tickets, run the show. It's still difficult—insurance alone is a nightmare—but it's a defined problem with a defined path.

The cooperative model doesn't work that way. It requires more people, more coordination, and more consensus before you even start. You're not organizing an event—you're organizing a movement. And movements take time.

#### What It Actually Requires

You need a founding group of 5-10 teams willing to commit not just to racing, but to building the system itself. These aren't participants; they're partners. They need to agree on governance structure, revenue splits, entry standards, and competitive philosophy before a single wrench gets turned or a single dollar changes hands.

You need lawyers who understand cooperative structures—member-owned LLCs, bylaws that protect against infiltration, contracts that enforce anti-affiliation rules. This isn't boilerplate stuff. It's custom legal architecture designed to survive pressure that doesn't exist yet.

You need accountants who can model revenue distribution formulas, calculate sustainable operational budgets, and project whether a 60/40 split or an 80/20 split makes more sense given realistic ticket sales and sponsor commitments. You need someone who can explain to a team whether a $15,000 buy-in is reasonable or exploitative.

You need bylaws—real, binding, enforceable bylaws—drafted and ratified by the founding members before Year 1. What counts as a major decision? How do you define affiliation? What's the process for expelling a member? How do sponsor standards get updated? This isn't vague mission-statement language. It's constitutional law.

All of this has to happen before the first race. And it has to happen through consensus, which means every founding team needs to understand not just what they're signing, but why it matters. That takes education, trust-building, and patience.

It's a lot.

#### This Requires A Different Kind of Commitment

If you're a team that just wants to race, this model asks more of you than showing up with a truck.

You're not entering someone else's event. You're co-creating a system. That means participating in governance—voting on rule changes, weighing in on entry standards, discussing revenue formulas, approving new members. It means reading bylaws, attending meetings (virtual or otherwise), and taking responsibility for decisions that affect everyone.

It means thinking like a stakeholder, not just a competitor. You're not renting access to someone else's infrastructure; you're building and maintaining the infrastructure yourself. That's more work. It's ongoing work. And it doesn't come with a promoter handling the messy stuff while you focus on your truck.

But in return, you get something no contractor ever gets: **ownership.**

You own your seat at the table. You own your vote. You own a share of the revenue the championship generates. You own the system that can't be sold, can't be bought, and can't be turned into something you didn't agree to. Nobody can take that from you as long as you meet your obligations.

If the championship grows, you grow with it—not as a hired performer hoping for a better contract next year, but as a member who shares in the collective success. If the sport builds long-term value, you own a piece of that value. And if someone tries to turn it into another Feld, you have the power to stop them.

That's the trade. More responsibility, more complexity—but real control and real security in return.

#### The Difficulty IS The Protection

The cooperative model is designed to be hard to corrupt, which means it's also hard to build.

A single promoter with a clear vision can make decisions quickly. They can pivot when something isn't working, cut deals without consensus, and move fast. That's an advantage—right up until that promoter decides to sell, or gets bought out, or starts optimizing for goals that conflict with sporting integrity. At that point, speed becomes liability. One person's decision can undo everything.

Democratic governance is slower. Messier. A rule change that one person could implement in a week might take the cooperative months to debate, refine, and ratify. Decisions require buy-in, compromise, and patience. It's inefficient by design.

But that inefficiency is the immune system.

You can't make a unilateral decision to sell the championship, because no individual owns it. You can't quietly change the competitive format to favor spectacle, because 75% of the members would have to agree. You can't let a corporate sponsor dictate the rules, because the teams control the bylaws. The friction—the votes, the transparency, the requirement for consensus—is what prevents any single actor from hijacking the system.

It's not fast. It's durable.

A benevolent dictator can build something great quickly. But benevolent dictators retire, get bought out, or stop being benevolent. The cooperative model survives because it doesn't rely on one person staying good. It survives because the structure makes corruption expensive, detectable, and ultimately futile.

You're not building the fastest path to success. You're building one that lasts.

#### The Filter Effect

This model will not appeal to everyone. And that's fine.

Some teams will hear "cooperative governance" and see bureaucracy. They'll hear "ownership disclosure" and think surveillance. They'll hear "supermajority votes" and imagine endless meetings arguing over minutiae. For those teams, this isn't the right fit. A traditional event—where a promoter handles everything and teams just show up to race—might serve them better.

But the teams who see cooperative governance and think _protection_—who hear "ownership stake" and see _control_—who understand that the complexity exists to prevent exactly what happened to the sport before—those are your founding members.

The difficulty filters for long-term thinkers. It selects for people who care more about building something sustainable than about convenience. It finds the teams who've been burned by centralized control and are willing to do the harder work to make sure it doesn't happen again.

That's not a bug. That's the point.

If ten teams read this and nine walk away, but the one who stays _gets it_—understands why the structure matters, why the bylaws exist, why the friction is essential—then you've found someone worth building with. Do that ten times and you have a founding group.

The cooperative model isn't trying to attract everyone. It's trying to attract the right people. And the complexity is how you find them.

**So Maybe This Isn't Day 1**

### **5. The Honest Reality: This Might Not Be Day 1**

I don't have all the answers here. I've never organized a monster truck show, let alone built a member-owned cooperative from scratch. I don't know if the cooperative model as described is immediately achievable, or if it needs refinement, or if someone with more experience will read this and realize there's a better way to accomplish the same goal.

What I do know is the principle: **independent teams need independent infrastructure they can't lose control of.** How that gets built—whether it's exactly as outlined here or some adapted version—matters less than whether it actually solves the problem.

#### Possible Paths Forward

Maybe this launches as a cooperative from Day 1. A group of 5-10 founding teams gets together, hires the lawyers and accountants, drafts the bylaws, agrees on governance and revenue models, and builds the whole thing collectively before the first event. It's the hardest path, but it's the cleanest. Everything is protected from the start, and there's no transition period where control could slip.

Or maybe it doesn't start that way. Maybe Year 1 is a well-run independent event—one promoter, clear rules, legitimate competition, serious prize money—that proves the concept works. Teams show up, fans show up, sponsors show up. It makes money, or at least breaks even, and demonstrates that there's a viable market for racing-first monster trucks. Then, in Year 2 or Year 3, the founder transitions ownership to the teams. The structure converts to a cooperative, bylaws get ratified, and the people who proved the model works inherit control of it.

This path is easier to launch but riskier. It requires trusting that the founder will actually hand over control when the time comes—and history suggests that's a hard thing to do when you've built something valuable. But if the founder is someone who understands why the cooperative model matters, who's committed to the mission over the profit, it could work. It just requires the right person in the right moment.

Or maybe someone smarter than me reads this and figures out a hybrid structure that accomplishes the same goal with different mechanics. Maybe there's a legal framework I'm not aware of that protects against consolidation without requiring full cooperative governance. Maybe the solution is simpler, or more robust, or just better thought out.

All of these paths are valid, as long as they solve the core problem: **preventing another entity from doing to independent monster trucks what Feld did to the sport the first time.**

#### What Matters Most

The principle matters more than the exact implementation.

Independent teams need to own, or at least control, the infrastructure they compete within. They need a system where the rules can't be changed unilaterally, where the format can't drift back toward spectacle, where success doesn't invite takeover. They need protection that's structural, not dependent on any individual's goodwill.

How that protection gets built—whether it's a cooperative, a different kind of member-owned entity, or something I haven't thought of—is less important than the fact that it exists and works.

The cooperative model as outlined here is one answer. It might be the right answer. It might need refinement. But even if it's not executed exactly as written, the conversation it starts matters. Because right now, there is no conversation about structural protection in independent monster truck racing. There's just a vague hope that someone will run a better show and it won't get co-opted.

Hope isn't a strategy. Structure is.

#### Starting The Conversation

Maybe someone reads this and says, "I can do this." Maybe they're a team owner with the connections to organize a founding group. Maybe they're a promoter who sees the value in transitioning to team ownership. Maybe they're a lawyer who knows how to build the legal framework, or an accountant who can model the financials, or just someone with enough credibility in the community to get people in a room and start talking.

Maybe it doesn't happen that cleanly. Maybe this document just plants a seed. Maybe it takes three years for the right people to find each other, another two years to organize, and Year 1 doesn't happen until 2030. That's fine. The sport has waited this long. It can wait for the right structure.

Or maybe someone reads this and adapts it into something different—keeps the principles, changes the mechanics, builds a better version. Maybe they see flaws I missed or solutions I didn't consider. That's not failure. That's progress.

The goal here isn't to execute this perfectly on Day 1. The goal is to move the conversation in the right direction—away from "how do we create a better show" and toward "how do we create a system that can't be corrupted."

If this document does that—if it gets even a handful of the right people thinking structurally instead of tactically—then it's done its job.

**So How Does This Fit With Everything Else?**

### **6. Why It Aligns With Everything Else**

This entire document has been a critique of centralized control.

The structural problems section showed how consolidation killed the sport—how centralization replaced competition with spectacle, how corporate optimization drained the danger and creativity that made monster trucks worth watching, how one entity controlling the infrastructure inevitably led to decisions that served profit over sport.

If that diagnosis is correct—if centralized control is what broke monster trucks—then the solution can't rely on centralized control, even benevolent centralized control.

You can't fix a structural problem with good intentions. You fix it with better structure.

The cooperative model is the only approach that's philosophically consistent with the critique. If the problem was that teams lost control of their sport, then the solution is giving them that control back—and building a system where they can't lose it again.

#### What Monster Trucks Were Built On

Monster trucks didn't come from a corporate boardroom. They came from independent builders in garages—people like Bob Chandler, Everett Jasmer, and dozens of others who built wild machines because they could, because no one told them not to, because the infrastructure didn't exist yet to stop them.

The golden era of the sport—the era people remember, the era that still draws nostalgic YouTube views at 2 a.m.—was decentralized, chaotic, and creative. Every team was independent. Every show was a little different. Builders experimented, drivers took risks, and the competition was real because no one was managing the outcomes.

Corporate consolidation is what killed that. Feld didn't destroy the sport out of malice—they optimized it, standardized it, and made it profitable. But in doing so, they paved over the independence that made it matter.

The solution has to honor those roots. Independent teams need independent infrastructure. Not infrastructure owned by a promoter who promises to be fair. Not infrastructure controlled by a corporation that promises to prioritize competition. **Infrastructure they own, collectively, and can't be taken from them.**

That's what the cooperative model does. It rebuilds the decentralized, builder-driven foundation the sport was born from—but with the legal and financial structure to survive in a modern commercial landscape.

#### The Alternative Is A Trap

A well-meaning promoter could absolutely build a better event than what exists today. They could prioritize racing, pay real prize money, treat teams with respect, and create something fans actually want to watch.

And for a few years, it might work beautifully.

But that promoter is still one decision away from becoming the next problem. They retire and sell to someone who doesn't share their values. They take on investors who demand higher returns. They get an acquisition offer they can't refuse. They simply get tired of losing money and start making compromises.

At that point, the teams who invested their time, money, and reputations have no recourse. They're back to being contractors in someone else's system, hoping the new boss is better than the last one.

Team ownership isn't a nice-to-have. It's not an idealistic flourish. It's the only structural mechanism that prevents the cycle from repeating.

#### Independent Teams Deserve Independent Infrastructure

That's what this is.

Not a event. Not a tour. Not a better version of what already exists.

A system. Infrastructure. A framework that independent teams own, control, and can't lose—because it's theirs, and no one else can take it.

That's the vision. Whether it happens exactly as outlined here, or gets adapted, or takes years to build—the principle stands.

Independent teams deserve independent infrastructure. And if enough people believe that, someone will build it.
